# The Little Bell

Takezo lay among thousands of dead bodies.
"The whole world is crazy," he thought. "A man is like a dead leaf blowing in the wind."

He looked like a dead body too. He tried to lift his head but could only move it a little. He felt very weak. "How long have I been here?" he wondered.

Flies buzzed around his head. He wanted to wave them away, but he was too tired to lift his arm. His body felt stiff. "I must have been asleep for a long time," he thought. He moved his fingers one by one. He did not know that he was hurt. Two bullets were stuck in his leg.

Dark clouds moved across the sky. The night before, heavy rain had fallen on the battlefield of Sekigahara. It was now past noon on September 15, 1600. The storm had ended, but rain still fell sometimes. When it rained, Takezo opened his mouth to catch the drops of water. "It is like the water given to a dying man," he thought. His head felt numb, and his mind was confused.

His army had lost the battle. He knew that for sure. Kobayakawa Hideaki was supposed to be their friend, but he secretly helped the other side. When Hideaki attacked his own friends, the western army lost. The fighting lasted only half a day. Now, a powerful man named Tokugawa Ieyasu would rule the country.

Takezo saw pictures of his sister and friends in his mind. "I am dying," he thought, but he did not feel sad. He felt relaxed, like he was ready to sleep forever.

Suddenly, one of the bodies next to him moved. "Takezo," a voice said.

Takezo stopped thinking and turned his head. He recognized the voice. It was his best friend. He tried hard to raise his head. "Matahachi, is that you?" he whispered. Then he fell back and listened.

"Takezo! Are you really alive?"

"Yes, I am alive!" Takezo shouted. "And you must not die either! Do not die!" He opened his eyes wide and smiled a little.

"I will not die!" Matahachi breathed heavily. He crawled slowly on his arms and dragged his stiff legs. He reached for Takezo's hand but only caught his little finger. When they were children, they often promised things by holding little fingers. He moved closer and held Takezo's whole hand.

"I am so glad you are safe! We might be the only ones still alive," Matahachi said.

"Do not say that yet. I have not tried to stand up," Takezo replied.

"I will help you. Let us leave this place!"

Suddenly, Takezo pulled Matahachi down. "Pretend to be dead! Trouble is coming!" he whispered loudly.

The ground began to shake. They watched a group of men on black horses running quickly toward them.

"The enemies are back!" Matahachi cried. He bent his knee to run. Takezo grabbed his leg hard and pulled him back to the ground.

Soon, hundreds of horses ran past them. The horses stepped on the dead bodies. The riders shouted, and their armor made loud noises.

Matahachi lay flat with his eyes closed. He hoped the horses would not step on them. Takezo looked up without blinking. The horses were so close that they could smell them. Then, the horses were gone.

By luck, they were safe. For a few minutes, they could not speak because they were so surprised.

"We are saved again!" Takezo said, holding out his hand. Matahachi kept his head down but smiled weakly. "Someone is protecting us," he said softly.

The two friends helped each other stand up. It was very hard. They walked slowly across the battlefield to the woods. They leaned on each other for support.

When they reached the trees, they fell down to rest. Later, they looked for food. For two days, they ate wild nuts and leaves on Mount Ibuki. They survived, but Takezo's stomach hurt, and Matahachi felt sick. They were still hungry and thirsty, but they started to feel stronger.

The storm was over. Two nights later, a bright, cold moon shone in the clear sky.

They knew it was dangerous to walk at night. In the bright moonlight, enemies could easily see them. But they had to keep moving because Matahachi was very sick. He said he would rather be caught than keep walking. They decided to risk it and walk toward a small town called Tarui. They also needed to find a safe place to rest.

"Can you keep going?" Takezo asked many times. He helped Matahachi walk. "Are you okay? Do you want to rest?"

"I am fine," Matahachi lied to sound brave. But his face was very pale. He used his spear to help him walk, but he could barely move.

He kept saying he was sorry. "I am sorry, Takezo. I am slowing us down. I am really sorry."

At first, Takezo said, "Do not worry about it." But later, when they rested, Takezo said, "I should be the one saying sorry. I brought you here. I wanted to do something great to impress my father. He always thought I was useless. I wanted to prove him wrong."

Takezo's father used to be a samurai for Lord Shimmen. When Takezo heard about the war, he thought it was his big chance. He wanted to be a samurai like his father. He wanted to fight and win so everyone in his village would respect him.

Matahachi nodded. "I know. But I wanted the same thing. It is not just your fault."

Takezo continued, "I wanted you to come with me because we are best friends. But your mother was very angry. She said I was crazy. Your future wife, Otsu, and my sister cried. They said we should stay home. Maybe they were right. If we die, our families will end. But I did not want to live a boring life."

They had left their village secretly. They thought they would become heroes easily. But when they joined the army, they learned the truth. The leaders did not care about who their fathers were. To the generals, they were just silly farm boys. The leaders only let them carry bags, cut grass, and do hard work. They were not real soldiers.

"Samurai! What a joke," Takezo said. "I did not even see an enemy leader. Well, it is over now. What should we do? I cannot leave you here. If I do, I will never be able to face your mother or Otsu again."

"Takezo, I do not blame you. We lost because of that traitor, Kobayakawa. I want to kill him!" Matahachi said.

Hours later, they stood near a field of tall grass. The storm had broken the grass. There were no houses or lights.

There were many dead bodies here too. The rain had washed the blood away. In the moonlight, their skin looked pale. Everything was quiet except for the sounds of bugs.

Tears fell down Matahachi's dirty face. He sighed deeply.

"Takezo, if I die, will you take care of Otsu?" he asked.

"What are you talking about?"

"I feel like I am dying."

Takezo got angry. "If you think like that, you will die." He wished his friend was stronger so he could feel hopeful. "Come on, Matahachi! Do not cry like a baby."

"My mother has people to help her, but Otsu is all alone. I feel bad for her. Promise you will help her if I die."

"Stop it! A stomachache does not kill you. We will find a house soon. Then I will get you a bed and some medicine. Stop talking about dying!"

They walked further and saw many more dead bodies. They were so used to the blood that they did not care anymore. They stopped to rest again.

While they rested, they heard something moving among the bodies. They were scared and hid quickly.

Someone was moving on the ground like a rabbit. They looked closely and saw it was a young girl. She was about thirteen or fourteen years old. She wore a pretty but torn dress with a gold belt. It was very strange to see a young girl in a field of dead bodies. She stared at them with careful eyes.

Takezo and Matahachi wondered why a girl was out here alone in the dark.

They stared at her for a moment. Then Takezo asked, "Who are you?"

She blinked, stood up, and ran away fast.

"Stop!" Takezo yelled. "I just want to ask you something! Do not go!"

But she was gone. They only heard the sound of a small bell ringing as she ran into the dark.

"Was that a ghost?" Takezo asked softly.

Matahachi laughed nervously. "If there are ghosts, they would be soldiers, right?"

"I wish I did not scare her," Takezo said. "She could have told us where to find a village."

They kept walking and climbed a hill. On the other side, they saw a light. It was only half a mile away.

They walked to the house. It looked special. It had a thick dirt wall around it and a large, old gate.

Takezo knocked softly on the door. "Is anyone home?"

No one answered. He knocked again. "I am sorry to bother you so late. My friend is sick and needs to rest. We do not want to cause trouble."

They heard whispering inside. Soon, someone walked to the door.

"You are soldiers running from the battle, right?" a young girl asked.

"Yes," Takezo said. "We fought for Lord Shimmen."

"Go away! If people find you here, we will be punished."

"Please, we have walked a long way. My friend just needs to rest, and—"

"Please leave!"

"Okay, we will go if you want. But can you give us some medicine? He is very sick, and it is hard for him to walk."

"I am not sure..."

They heard footsteps moving away. The sound of a little bell rang inside the house.

Then they saw a face in the window. It was a woman watching them.

"Akemi," she said to the girl, "let them in. They are just foot soldiers. The enemies will not look for them."

The girl, Akemi, opened the door. The woman said her name was Oko. She listened to Takezo's story.

Oko let them sleep in the shed where they kept wood. She gave Matahachi medicine made from charcoal and some warm rice soup to help his stomach. For the next few days, Matahachi slept a lot. Takezo stayed with him and used cheap alcohol to clean the bullet wounds in his own leg.

About a week later, Takezo and Matahachi were talking in the evening.

"They must sell something to survive," Takezo said.

"I do not care. I am just happy they helped us," Matahachi replied.

But Takezo was curious. "The mother is not very old," he said. "It is strange that two women live here alone."

"Do you think the girl looks like Otsu?" Matahachi asked.

"A little bit, but they do not really look alike. They are both pretty, that is all. Why was she walking among the dead bodies that night? She did not look scared at all. She looked very calm."

Matahachi told him to be quiet. "Shh! I hear her bell."

Akemi knocked softly on the door. "Matahachi, Takezo," she said.

"Yes?"

"It is me."

Takezo opened the door. She brought them food and medicine. "How do you feel?" she asked.

"Much better, thank you and your mother," Takezo said.

"Mother says you must stay quiet and not go outside, even if you feel better."

"We are sorry to cause trouble," Takezo said.

"It is fine, but you must be careful. The enemies are still looking for General Ishida. There are many enemy soldiers on the roads."

"Really?"

"Yes. So Mother says if they find us hiding you, we will be in trouble too."

"We will be very quiet," Takezo promised. "I will cover Matahachi's mouth if he snores."

Akemi smiled. "Good night. I will see you tomorrow."

"Wait!" Matahachi said. "Stay and talk to us."

"I cannot."

"Why not?"

"Mother will be mad."

"Why do you care? How old are you?"

"Sixteen."

"You are small for your age."

"Thanks," she said sarcastically.

"Where is your father?"

"I do not have one."

"I am sorry. How do you make money?"

"We make moxa medicine."

"The medicine you burn to stop pain?"

"Yes. People know our moxa is the best. In spring, we pick the plants. In summer, we dry them. In fall and winter, we make the medicine safely here. We sell it in Tarui. People come from far away to buy it."

"You do not need a man to help you," Matahachi said.

"If that is all, I must go."

"Wait one more second," Takezo said. "I have a question."

"What?"

"The night we arrived, we saw a girl looking at the dead bodies. She looked just like you. That was you, right?"

Akemi turned quickly and opened the door.

"What were you doing there?" Takezo asked.

She slammed the door. As she ran back to the house, the little bell rang strangely in the dark.
