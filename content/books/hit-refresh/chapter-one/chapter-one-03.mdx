This is a book about transformation. This transformation is happening inside me and inside our company. It is driven by empathy and a desire to empower others. Most importantly, it is about the change coming to every life. We are witnessing a huge wave of technology. This includes artificial intelligence, mixed reality, and quantum computing. People, organizations, and societies must transform. They must "hit refresh" to find new energy and ideas. At the core, this book is about humans and empathy. Empathy will become very valuable. Technology will disrupt our world like never before. The poet Rainer Maria Rilke wrote that the future enters us to transform us before it happens. Poetry can teach us as much as computer code. Rilke means that our future is inside us. It is determined by the choices we make today. I want to describe these choices.

In this book, you will follow three stories. First, I will share my own story. I moved from India to America. I lived in the Midwest and Silicon Valley. Then I joined Microsoft when it was growing fast. Second, I will talk about how we "hit refresh" at Microsoft. I became the CEO after Bill Gates and Steve Ballmer. Our transformation is not finished, but I am proud of our progress. Third, I will discuss the Fourth Industrial Revolution. In the future, machine intelligence will rival human intelligence. We will ask big questions. What will humans do? Will inequality get better or worse? How can governments help? What should global companies do? How will our society transform?

I was excited to write this book, but also a little hesitant. I wondered who would care about my story. I have only been Microsoft's CEO for a few years. It felt too early to write about our successes and failures. We have made progress, but we have a long way to go. I also did not want to write a memoir yet. I will save that for when I am old. However, a few things convinced me to write now. I felt a responsibility to share our story from my perspective. We are also going through a time of huge social and economic change. This change comes from new technologies like cloud computing, AI, mixed reality, and robotics. It feels like science fiction. People disagree about what this means for our future. Some think robots will do all the hard work while humans relax, like in the movie WALL-E. Others, like scientist Stephen Hawking, think it will end badly.

My biggest reason to write is for my colleagues at Microsoft and our customers. On the day I became CEO in 2014, I focused on our company culture. I said we needed to find Microsoft's soul again. I believe my main job is to guide our culture. This helps our 100,000 employees shape our future. Usually, leaders write books after they retire. I wanted to write while we are still in the middle of this big change. Microsoft's original goal was to put "a computer on every desk and in every home." This mission shaped our culture. Now, almost everyone has a computer and a smartphone. We succeeded in many ways, but we were falling behind in others. PC sales were slow. We were behind in mobile phones and search engines. We needed to grow our gaming business again. We needed to understand our customers better. It was time to "hit refresh."

I worked at Microsoft as an engineer and a leader for 22 years. I was calm when they were looking for a new CEO. Many people guessed who would take Steve Ballmer's place. My wife, Anu, and I mostly ignored the rumors. We were busy taking care of our son Zain and our two daughters. At work, I was focused on growing the Microsoft Cloud business. I thought the board would choose the best person. I would be happy if they chose me. But I would also be happy to work for someone else. During the interviews, one board member told me I needed to show I really wanted the job. I thought about it and talked to Steve. He laughed and said, "It's too late to be different." It was not my style to show obvious ambition.

On January 24, 2014, John Thompson emailed me. He was leading the CEO search. He asked for time to chat. I did not know what to expect. I thought he would just give me an update on the search. When he called that evening, he asked if I was sitting down. I was not. I was playing with a cricket ball while talking on speakerphone. He told me I was the new CEO of Microsoft. It took me a few minutes to process the news. I told him I was honored, humbled, and excited. I meant every word. Weeks later, I told the press that we needed to focus better, move faster, and change our culture. But secretly, I knew I had to answer some big questions first. Why does Microsoft exist? Why am I in this new role? Everyone should ask themselves these questions. Every person and company must eventually "hit refresh" to find their purpose again. They have to reenergize and rethink. It is not as easy as refreshing a web browser. But when done right, it leads to a rebirth. Other companies have done it, and more will have to do it in the future.

Let me start at the beginning of my story. Why do I ask such big questions about why we exist? Why are culture, ideas, and empathy so important to me? My father was a civil servant who liked Marxist ideas. My mother studied Sanskrit, an ancient language. I learned about history and intellectual curiosity from my father. But I was always my mother's son. She wanted me to be happy, confident, and live in the moment. She worked hard at home and taught Indian philosophy at a college. She made our home full of joy.

My earliest memories are of my parents. My mom worked hard at her job and her marriage. She was my steady force. My father was larger than life. He almost moved to the United States to study economics. But he changed his plans when he joined the Indian Administrative Service (IAS). This was in the early 1960s, after India became independent from Great Britain. It was an exciting time to join the government and build a new nation. Only a few young professionals were chosen for the IAS each year. My father managed a large district with millions of people at a young age. We moved around a lot in the state of Andhra Pradesh. I grew up in old colonial buildings with lots of space, watching my country change.
