It was a cool November day. The year was 1992. I visited the Microsoft campus in the Pacific Northwest for the first time. I entered Building 22. It was a normal corporate office building. Tall Douglas fir trees covered the area. You can barely see it from state route 520. This road has a floating bridge that connects Seattle to Redmond. Microsoft's stock price was starting a huge rise. Its founders, Bill Gates and Paul Allen, could still walk down the street without being noticed.

It was an exciting time in technology. Windows 3.1 was just released. This prepared the market for Windows 95. Sony introduced the CD-ROM. The first website was also launched. The Internet would become huge two years later. Cable TV companies brought digital cable. The FCC approved digital radio. Personal computer sales were going up very fast. I joined at the perfect time. Microsoft had the money, smart people, and ideas to lead the industry.

My path to Redmond was long. I moved from India to Wisconsin for graduate school. Then, I went to Silicon Valley to work at Sun Microsystems. During the summer, Microsoft asked me to work for them. I was 25 years old. My job was to tell people about Windows NT. This was a new 32-bit operating system. It was built for powerful business computers. Later, NT became the base for all future Windows versions, including Windows 10. While I was at Sun, I heard of NT but never used it. A coworker saw NT at a Microsoft event and told me about it.

I wanted a job with a big impact. Richard Tait and Jeff Teper hired me at Microsoft. They needed someone who knew about UNIX and 32-bit systems. I was not sure about taking the job. I really wanted to go to business school. Management skills would mix well with my engineering skills. I was thinking about working in investment banking. I was accepted into the full-time program at the University of Chicago. But Jeff Teper told me to join Microsoft right away. I decided to do both. I changed to the part-time Chicago program. I flew to Chicago on weekends, but I didn't tell anyone.

I finished my MBA in two years. I am happy that I did it. During the week, I flew across the country with heavy Compaq computers. I met with leaders at big companies like Georgia Pacific and Mobil. I tried to make them buy our new business operating system. On the weekends, I studied at school. I actually learned more math in my advanced finance classes than in my engineering classes. My teachers taught strategy, finance, and leadership. Their lessons changed my thinking for a long time.

It was fun to work at Microsoft. Soon after I started, I met Steve Ballmer. He came to my office and gave me a big high five. He was happy that I left Sun to join Microsoft. That was the first of many great talks with Steve. People at the company had a lot of energy and a clear goal. We felt like we could do anything.

A few years later, my work with Windows NT led me to a new group. Nathan Myhrvold started this advanced technology group. Microsoft had many smart people like Rick Rashid and Craig Mundie. It felt like the smartest technology group since Xerox PARC. I was proud to become a product manager there. Our project was called Tiger Server. It was a big effort to build a video-on-demand service. This was years before cable companies offered on-demand video. It was also long before Netflix became popular.

I lived near the Microsoft campus. The campus had a fast network for our video project. In 1994, I was able to watch video-on-demand in my apartment. We only had about 15 movies, but I watched them many times. Our team wanted to bring the Tiger Server straight to people's homes. We planned to use an ATM network. However, the Internet grew very fast and made our idea old overnight.

While I worked hard, I was also thinking about love. Anu and I decided to get married before I started at Microsoft. I knew Anu my whole life. Her dad and my dad were friends from their government jobs. Anu's dad and I loved talking about cricket. We still talk about it today. He played cricket in school and college. It is hard to say exactly when I fell in love with Anu. Our families were close. We played together as children. We went to the same schools. Our family dogs were from the same litter.

When I moved to America, we lost touch. Then, I visited India and saw her again. She was finishing her architecture degree at Manipal. She was working in New Delhi. Our families had dinner together. That night, I knew I wanted to marry her. We had the same values and dreams.

The next day, I asked her to take me to get my glasses fixed. After that, we walked around Lodi Gardens for hours. It is an old, famous place in Delhi. Anu loved historical places, and we explored many of them. We had lunch and visited a drama school. We bought books in Khan Market. I proposed in Lodi Gardens on an October afternoon in 1992. She said yes. We told her mother and got married two months later in December. It was a happy time, but visa issues caused problems.

Anu needed to finish her architecture degree in India. In 1993, she tried to get a visa to visit me during her break. Her visa was denied because I was a permanent U.S. resident. Her father met with a U.S. official in New Delhi. He said the visa rules hurt families. The official was kind and gave Anu a short tourist visa. After the visit, Anu went back to India to finish school. We realized it would be very hard for her to return to the U.S.

The wait for spouses of permanent residents was very long. A Microsoft lawyer told me it could take five years for Anu to move. I thought about quitting my job and going back to India. Then, the lawyer gave me an idea. He said I should give up my green card and get a temporary H1B visa. The H1B visa lets spouses come to the U.S. right away. This did not make sense, but it was the law. Making the choice was easy because Anu was my priority.

In June 1994, I went to the U.S. embassy in Delhi. I told the worker I wanted to give back my green card and get an H1B visa. He was very surprised. He asked me why, and gave me a new form to fill out. The next day, I applied for the H1B visa. It worked. Anu moved to Seattle to stay with me. We started our family there.

At work, everyone talked about what I did. They knew me as the guy who gave up his green card. People often asked me for advice about visas. Years later, my friend Kunal Bahl left Microsoft because of his visa. He went to India and started Snapdeal. His company is now worth over $1 billion. Cloud companies like Snapdeal became very important for Microsoft. The lessons I learned in India still guide me today.
