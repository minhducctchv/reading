My mom tried her best to balance everything. She managed her teaching career, raised me, and was a loving wife. 

When I was about six years old, my five-month-old sister died. This event had a huge impact on our family. 

After that, my mom had to stop working. I think my sister's death was simply too much for her. She was already busy raising me and managing a career. My father was often working far away. 

She never complained to me about this. However, I often think about her story. I think about it especially when discussing diversity in the technology industry today. 

Like anyone else, she wanted and deserved to have both a career and a family. But the workplace culture and Indian social norms at the time made this impossible. She could not balance her family life with her career passions.

The children of IAS fathers often faced a lot of pressure to succeed. Some IAS fathers believed passing the hard entrance test set them up for life. They thought it was the last test they needed to take. 

My father was different. He saw the IAS exam as just the beginning. He was a person who loved to learn throughout his life. 

Many of my peers had parents who pushed them hard to achieve. Luckily, I did not experience that pressure. 

My mom was not a strict "tiger mom." She never pushed me to achieve specific goals. She only wanted me to be happy.

I was perfectly fine with that. As a child, I did not care much about anything except playing cricket. 

One day, my father put up a poster of Karl Marx in my bedroom. In response, my mother hung a poster of Lakshmi, the Indian goddess of wealth and happiness. 

Their messages were very different. My father wanted me to have high intellectual goals. My mother preferred that I simply be happy and free from strict beliefs. 

What did I want? I only wanted a poster of my cricket hero, M. L. Jaisimha. He was a famous player from Hyderabad, known for his good looks and graceful style both on and off the field.

Looking back, I see the influence of both my parents. My father's love for learning and my mother's wish for a balanced life shaped me. 

Even today, cricket is my greatest passion. The love for cricket is stronger in India than anywhere else, even though the game started in England. 

I played well enough to represent my school in Hyderabad. This city has a rich history and love for cricket. I played as an off-spin bowler. In baseball, this is like a pitcher who throws a sharp curveball. 

Cricket has about 2.5 billion fans around the world. In comparison, baseball has half a billion fans. Both are wonderful sports with dedicated fans. 

Both sports also have many books written about their beauty, excitement, and complexity. In his novel "Netherland," Joseph O’Neill wrote about the beauty of cricket. He described the eleven players moving together towards the batsman and then going back. He compared this repeating movement to the field breathing. 

Now, as a CEO, I often think of this cricket metaphor. It helps me understand the team culture we need to succeed.

I went to schools in various parts of India. I studied in Srikakulam, Tirupati, Mussoorie, Delhi, and Hyderabad. Each place left a lasting memory on me. 

For example, Mussoorie is a city in northern India. It sits in the lower mountains of the Himalayas, at about six thousand feet high. Whenever I look at Mount Rainier from my home in Bellevue, I remember the mountains from my childhood. 

I went to kindergarten at the Convent of Jesus and Mary. This is the oldest girls' school in India, but they allowed boys in kindergarten. 

When I was fifteen, we finally stopped moving. I joined the Hyderabad Public School (HPS). This school had students from all over India living there. 

I am glad we moved so much; it taught me how to adjust to new places easily. However, moving to Hyderabad had the biggest impact on me. 

In the 1970s, Hyderabad was a quiet place. It was not the huge city of 6.8 million people it is today. At the time, I did not care much about the rest of the world. But going to boarding school at HPS was the best thing that ever happened to me.

At HPS, I was placed in the Nalanda "house." This group was named after an ancient Buddhist university. 

The school had students from many different backgrounds. Muslims, Hindus, Christians, and Sikhs all lived and studied together. 

Children from very wealthy families went there. But there were also children from poor rural areas who attended on scholarships. The son of a top politician studied next to the children of movie stars. 

In my dormitory, there were boys from all economic backgrounds. The school treated everyone equally. It was a very special and memorable time.

Many successful people graduated from this school. The list includes the CEOs of Adobe and MasterCard, and the founders of major technology and financial companies. Politicians, movie stars, athletes, and writers also came from this small school. 

I was not a top student, and the school did not force strict academic rules. Students could choose what they wanted to learn. 

If you wanted to learn physics, you studied physics. If you found science boring and preferred history, you chose history instead. There was no intense pressure from peers to follow one specific career path.

After a few years at HPS, my father got a job at the United Nations in Bangkok. He did not like my relaxed attitude towards studying. 

He told me he wanted to move me to an international school in Bangkok for my last two years of high school. I completely refused. I decided to stay in Hyderabad. 

Many people thought I was crazy to make this choice. However, I was determined not to leave. Cricket was a huge part of my life then. Going to that school gave me wonderful memories and a lot of self-confidence.

By my final year of high school, my dream was simple. I wanted to attend a small college, play cricket for Hyderabad, and eventually work in a bank. 

I never thought about becoming an engineer or moving to the West. My mother supported my simple plans. 

However, my father strongly disagreed. He told me I had to leave Hyderabad or I would ruin my future. 

Looking back, it was good advice, even though no one knew Hyderabad would soon become a major technology city. It was hard to leave my close friends, but my father was right. My goals were too narrow and limited.

I needed to see the bigger picture. Cricket was my main passion, but I also loved computers. 

When I was fifteen, my father bought me a Sinclair computer kit from Bangkok. This computer used a processor built by an engineer who had previously worked at Intel. Interestingly, that Intel processor was the same one Bill Gates and Paul Allen used to create early Microsoft software. 

This little computer inspired me. It made me think about software and engineering. I also realized that computers could bring people together. I believed that if a kid in a remote part of India could learn to code, anyone in the world could.

I failed the entrance exam for the Indian Institutes of Technology (IIT). This exam was considered the ultimate goal for students like me in India. My father, who always passed his exams easily, simply laughed at my failure. 

Fortunately, I had two other options to study engineering. I was accepted to study mechanical engineering at one university and electrical engineering at another. I chose the electrical engineering program at the Manipal Institute of Technology. 

I guessed that electrical engineering would involve more computer learning. Luckily, I was right. This academic choice eventually led me to Silicon Valley and Microsoft. 

The friends I made in college were very ambitious and driven. I learned a lot from them. Years later, we even lived together in California, recreating our college dorm experience. 

However, my sports life changed at Manipal. Cricket was no longer my main passion. I only played one game for my college team before quitting. 

Computers replaced cricket as the most important thing in my life. At college, I learned exactly how computer chips and circuits were made.
