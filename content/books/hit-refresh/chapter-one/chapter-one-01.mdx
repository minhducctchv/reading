# Chapter 1: From Hyderabad to Redmond

## How Karl Marx, a Sanskrit Scholar, and a Cricket Hero Shaped My Boyhood

I joined Microsoft in 1992. I wanted to work for a company that wanted to change the world. That was twenty-five years ago. I have never regretted my choice.

Microsoft started the PC Revolution. Our success was legendary. It was similar to IBM's success in the past. But after many years of winning, things began to change. These changes were not good.

Bureaucracy replaced innovation. Internal politics replaced teamwork. We were falling behind our competitors.

During this time, a cartoonist drew a picture of Microsoft's organization chart. It showed different groups pointing guns at each other. The message was very clear. I have worked at Microsoft for twenty-four years, so this picture really bothered me.

What upset me more was that our employees just accepted it. I had seen some of these problems in my different jobs. But I believed we could solve them.

I became the third CEO of Microsoft in February 2014. I told the employees that changing our culture was my top priority. I wanted to remove the barriers to innovation. I wanted us to get back to our main goal: making a difference in the world.

Microsoft is best when it connects personal passion to a bigger purpose. Products like Windows, Office, Xbox, and Surface are examples of this. They are platforms that help people and organizations build their dreams.

We had achieved a lot, but I knew we could do more. Our employees were hungry for more success. I wanted Microsoft's culture to follow these values.

Soon after I became CEO, I changed how we held one of our most important meetings. Every week, my senior leadership team (SLT) meets. We talk about big opportunities and difficult decisions.

The SLT has many talented people. There are engineers, researchers, managers, and marketers. They come from many different backgrounds. They joined Microsoft because they love technology and want to make a difference.

At the time, the team included many leaders. Peggy Johnson leads business development. Kathleen Hogan leads human resources and helps me change our culture. Kurt Delbene leads our strategy. Qi Lu ran our applications and services business. Amy Hood is our CFO. Brad Smith is our president and legal officer. Scott Guthrie leads our cloud business. Terry Myerson was in charge of Windows. Chris Capossela is our chief marketing officer. Kevin Turner was the chief operating officer. Harry Shum leads Artificial Intelligence and research.

I was once a member of the SLT when Steve Ballmer was CEO. I admired everyone, but I felt we needed to understand each other better. I wanted us to connect our personal beliefs to our work as leaders.

I knew that if we stopped fighting and worked together, we could achieve our dream. This dream was to make advanced technology available to everyone.

Before I became CEO, the Seattle Seahawks won the Super Bowl. Their story inspired many of us. Their coach, Pete Carroll, hired a psychologist named Dr. Michael Gervais. Dr. Gervais teaches mindfulness to help people perform better.

He worked with the Seahawks to help players and coaches focus. This helped them succeed on and off the field. Like athletes, we all work in high-pressure environments. I thought our team could learn from this approach.

One Friday morning, the SLT met in a different place. We did not use the formal boardroom. Instead, we met in a relaxed space on campus used by developers. It was open and simple.

There were no tables or chairs. We did not use computers to check emails. We put our phones away. We sat on comfortable couches in a circle. There was no place to hide.

I asked everyone to stay in the moment and not judge others. I was hopeful but also a bit nervous.

For the first exercise, Dr. Gervais asked if we wanted an extraordinary experience. We all said yes. Then he asked for a volunteer to stand up. No one stood up at first. It was very quiet and awkward.

Then our CFO, Amy Hood, stood up. She was asked to say the alphabet while saying a number between each letter (like A1, B2, C3).

Dr. Gervais asked why others did not volunteer. We were a high-performing group that wanted to do something extraordinary. But we just looked at our shoes or smiled nervously.

The answers were hard to say. We felt fear. We were afraid of being laughed at or failing. We were afraid of not looking smart. Some felt arrogant and thought the game was stupid.

But Dr. Gervais was encouraging. People began to relax and laugh. The morning became brighter. One by one, we all spoke.

We shared our personal passions and beliefs. We reflected on who we are at home and at work. We talked about how we connect our personal lives with our jobs.

People talked about their spirituality and their families. They shared their dedication to making great products.

I realized this was the first time I heard my colleagues talk about themselves, not just business. I even saw some people with tears in their eyes.
