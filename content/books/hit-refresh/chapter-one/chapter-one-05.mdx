I didn't have a clear plan after finishing my electrical engineering degree.

My mother's philosophy of life influenced my thoughts about my future. She always believed in doing your own thing at your own pace. If you enjoy what you do, do it well, and have an honest purpose, life will not fail you. This belief has helped me all my life.

After graduation, I had a chance to attend a top industrial engineering institute in Bombay. I had also applied to colleges in the United States. At that time, getting a student visa was difficult. Honestly, I hoped my visa would be rejected because I never wanted to leave India.

However, I got the visa. I had to choose between staying in India or going to the University of Wisconsin at Milwaukee. A good friend from my old school was studying computer science there. Because of him, I made my decision. I joined the master's program in computer science at Wisconsin.

I am glad I chose that program. It was a small department. The professors really cared about their students. I am especially thankful to the department chair, Dr. Vairavan, and my advisor, Professor Hosseini. They taught me to tackle the biggest and hardest problems in computer science instead of choosing the easy path.

I could not point to Milwaukee on a map. But on my twenty-first birthday in 1988, I flew from New Delhi to Chicago. A friend drove me to the campus. I remember how quiet everything was. Milwaukee was stunning and beautiful. I thought it was heaven on earth. It was summer, and my life in America was just beginning.

Then, summer turned into winter. The cold in Wisconsin is shocking if you come from southern India. I used to smoke back then. All smokers had to stand outside. Many of us were from different parts of the world. The Indian students could not handle the cold, so we quit smoking. Then my Chinese friends also quit. However, the Russian students didn't care about the cold and kept smoking.

I got homesick sometimes, but America was very welcoming. My story would not be possible anywhere else, and I am proud to be an American citizen today.

Looking back, my story might sound very typical. The son of an Indian government worker studies hard, gets a degree, moves to the US, and succeeds in technology. However, it was not that simple. I was not a top student. I did not go to the elite Indian Institutes of Technology. In America, I got the chance to prove myself instead of being judged by my school.

I was very lucky to benefit from several historical events. These were India's independence, the American civil rights movement, and the global tech boom. India's independence improved education for people like me. In the US, a 1965 law changed immigration rules. Before that, very few Indians could immigrate. The new law allowed skilled workers to come to America. It favored people with technical training. Because of these events, I arrived in the US with software skills right before the tech boom in the 1990s. I felt like I hit the lottery.

During my first semester at Wisconsin, I took classes in image processing, computer architecture, and a programming language called LISP. The first assignments were huge programming projects. I was not a great coder at the time. The stereotype is that Indians are born to code, but everyone has to start somewhere. The work was tough, and I had to learn fast. Once I did, it was awesome.

I quickly realized that the microcomputer would change the world. At first, I thought it was all about making computer chips. Most of my friends specialized in chip design and worked at big companies.

I became very interested in a theoretical part of computer science. It focused on making fast decisions when there is uncertainty and limited time. I focused on a puzzle called graph coloring. This is a math problem where you assign labels to items based on certain rules. For example, imagine coloring a map of the US so that no two connected states have the same color. What is the least number of colors you need?

My master's thesis was about finding ways to quickly solve very complex graph coloring problems. It was about solving puzzles with endless possibilities in a fast and good way, even if the answer is not perfect.

I loved theoretical computer science because it showed the limits of modern computers. It also made me interested in famous scientists like John Von Neumann and Alan Turing, and in quantum computing. Learning how to manage problems with constraints was great training for my future role as a CEO.

I finished my master's degree and started working for a software company. I built applications for databases while writing my thesis. I became very good at database programming. During this time, technology was changing. Computers were moving from simple text screens to visual screens like Windows. This was early 1990. I didn't think about Microsoft because I used more powerful computers called workstations, not regular PCs.

In 1990, I moved to Silicon Valley for my first job at Sun Microsystems. Sun made the best workstations. Sun had many talented people working there.

During my two years at Sun, the computer industry was changing a lot. Both Sun and Microsoft wanted what the other company had. I was in the right place at the right time. Sun had me work on desktop software, like their email tool. I also worked with another company to bring their software to Sun computers.

However, I noticed a problem. Every few months, Sun changed its software strategy. This forced me to rewrite my code over and over again. Sun had great leaders, but they struggled to keep a clear software plan.

By 1992, I had to make some big choices. I wanted to build software that would change the world. I also wanted to go back to school to get a business degree. Most of all, I missed Anu. I wanted to marry her and bring her to America. She was finishing her college degree in India at the time.

I still had no master plan. But one afternoon, a phone call from Redmond, Washington, brought a new, unexpected opportunity. It was time to hit refresh again.
