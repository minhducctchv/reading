When it was my turn to speak, I felt many emotions. I thought about my life. I thought about my parents, my wife, and my children. I also thought about my work. My journey to this point was very long.

I remembered my childhood in India. I remembered moving to the United States as a young man. I thought about being a husband and a father to a child with special needs. I also thought about my work as an engineer. I design technology for billions of people. I even remembered my dream of being a professional cricket player. All these experiences help me in my new role. This role requires all my passions and values. Our challenges at Microsoft will require everyone's best efforts.

I told the team that work must have deep meaning. We spend a lot of time at work. We can achieve a lot if we connect our personal values with the company's goals. I have always wanted to learn. I learn from poetry, friends, and teachers. My goal is to connect new ideas with empathy for others. Ideas are exciting, but empathy keeps me balanced.

Years ago, a lack of empathy almost stopped me from joining Microsoft. During my interview, I met Richard Tait. He did not give me a hard engineering problem. He did not ask about my school. Instead, he asked one simple question.

"Imagine you see a baby crying in the street. What do you do?" he asked.

"I would call 911," I answered quickly.

Richard walked me to the door. He put his arm around me. He said, "You need empathy. If a baby is crying on the street, pick up the baby."

I still got the job, but I never forgot his words. Later, I learned about empathy in a very personal way.

Our first child, Zain, was born a few years later. My wife, Anu, and I were very excited. Anu's mother helped us prepare the house. We were happy and healthy. We wondered when Anu would return to her job as an architect. We thought about how our vacations would change.

One night, Anu noticed the baby was not moving much. She was in her 36th week of pregnancy. We went to the emergency room. We thought it was just a small problem. I was even annoyed by the wait time. However, the doctors were worried. They performed an emergency surgery. Zain was born on August 13, 1996. He weighed only three pounds. He did not cry.

Zain was moved to Seattle Children’s Hospital for special care. Anu stayed in the hospital to recover. I went to see Zain the next morning. Our lives changed completely. We learned that Zain had brain damage. He had severe cerebral palsy. He would need a wheelchair and our help forever. I was very sad.

Anu helped me change my perspective. She said it was not about us. It was about understanding Zain. We needed to feel empathy for his pain. We had to accept our responsibility as his parents.

Being a husband and a father changed me. I now understand people with different abilities better. I also learned about love and human creativity. During this time, I studied the teachings of Gautama Buddha. I am not religious, but I was curious. Buddha wanted to understand why people suffer.

I learned that life's ups and downs create empathy. To suffer less, we must accept that things change. At first, I was upset that Zain's condition would never change. But I realized that everything changes over time. If we understand this, we can stay calm. Then we can feel empathy and compassion for everyone. I liked this simple lesson for life.

I am not perfect. I am not enlightened. But my life has taught me to feel empathy for many people. I feel empathy for people with disabilities. I feel empathy for people in poor cities and developing countries. I feel empathy for small business owners. I also feel empathy for anyone facing hate or violence. I want to put empathy at the center of everything I do. This includes our products and how we treat our employees and customers.

Technology can improve lives. At home, three high school students built an app for Zain. Zain loves music, but he could not control it himself. He had to wait for help. These students used a sensor on his wheelchair. Now, Zain can tap his head to change the music. This gave him freedom and happiness.

This same empathy inspires me at work. I told my team about a project at Microsoft. We created technology that tracks eye movement. This helps people with ALS and cerebral palsy. They can be more independent. The idea came from an employee hackathon. One team spent time with Steve Gleason. He is a former football player with ALS. Technology helps him every day. I know how important this is for people like Steve and my son.

Our leadership team began to change that day. We felt a higher purpose. We wanted to use Microsoft to help others. It was an emotional day. We realized that one person cannot save Microsoft. We must all work together. Changing the company's culture will be slow and difficult, but it will be worth it.
